Oscillatory Systems and the Dynamics of Restoring Forces

A mathematical treatment of simple harmonic oscillation, phase-space behaviour, energetic interchange and the approximation of nonlinear restoring systems.

7.1 The Harmonic Approximation

An oscillatory system is conventionally characterised by the existence of a dynamically stable equilibrium configuration. When sufficiently small perturbations are applied to such a system, the resultant restoring force may frequently be approximated by the first non-vanishing term of its Taylor expansion about the equilibrium coordinate.

For a one-dimensional displacement x, the linearised restoring force consequently assumes the form

F(x) = −kx

where k denotes an effective stiffness parameter. Substitution into Newton's second law yields the canonical differential equation governing simple harmonic motion,

d²x/dt² + ω²x = 0

with the characteristic angular frequency satisfying ω² = k/m. The corresponding displacement may therefore be expressed as a sinusoidal function whose phase is determined by the initial conditions imposed upon the oscillator.

7.2 Phase Evolution

The instantaneous mechanical state of an ideal harmonic oscillator may be represented within phase space by the ordered pair consisting of displacement and conjugate velocity. Eliminating time between the displacement and velocity equations produces a closed elliptical trajectory.

This representation is particularly useful because it removes the explicit temporal dependence of the motion and instead provides a geometrical description of the system's dynamical evolution.

Figure 7.2 — Phase-space trajectory of an undamped harmonic oscillator. Conservation of mechanical energy restricts the state vector to a closed orbit.

7.3 Energy Interchange

Although the total mechanical energy of an isolated harmonic oscillator remains invariant, its kinetic and potential components undergo continuous reciprocal transformation. Maximum kinetic energy occurs as the oscillator traverses its equilibrium position, whereas maximum elastic potential energy corresponds to the turning points of the trajectory.

E = ½mv² + ½kx² = constant
Remark. The invariance of total mechanical energy is contingent upon the absence of dissipative interactions. Introduction of even weak velocity-dependent damping destroys the closed phase-space orbit and produces an inward spiral toward equilibrium.

7.4 Weakly Damped Motion

In experimentally realised oscillatory systems, resistive interactions frequently introduce an approximately velocity-dependent force. The resulting equation of motion may be written

m(d²x/dt²) + b(dx/dt) + kx = 0

where b represents the phenomenological damping coefficient. In the underdamped regime, the system retains an oscillatory response, although its amplitude decreases exponentially with time.

This behaviour constitutes an important departure from ideal harmonic motion and provides a useful approximation to numerous mechanical, electrical and molecular systems encountered in experimental physics.
